The Service

What ground-up commercial construction involves

New commercial construction runs in phases, and each one has to be complete and inspected before the next can safely start: site work and foundations, structure and envelope, mechanical and electrical rough-in, interior build-out, and closeout. The sequence is unforgiving, which is why so much of the outcome is decided during preconstruction rather than on site.

We work with your design team to turn drawings into a buildable scope and a realistic schedule, then manage the trades and inspections through delivery. If your project is a build-out within an existing building rather than a new structure, tenant improvements and build-outs covers that, and commercial general contracting and project management explains the oversight role in more detail. Exterior surfaces and parking are covered under commercial concrete, asphalt, and parking lot work.

Is This You?

When building new is the right call

Ground-up construction is a bigger commitment than adapting an existing property, and it makes sense when the alternative genuinely does not work. Common situations include:

  • You have outgrown the current propertyThe business needs more space than any adaptation of the existing building can deliver.
  • You have acquired landYou own or are buying a site and want to build rather than continue leasing.
  • You need a purpose-built facilityThe operation has requirements that a general-purpose building cannot reasonably be adapted to meet.
  • Adapting would cost more than buildingThe existing structure needs so much work that new construction becomes the more sensible route.

Building in Bakersfield

New commercial construction across Bakersfield and Kern County

Kern County sites vary widely in access, existing services, and what the ground itself will support, and those factors shape the early phases far more than the finishes do. Local inspection scheduling also sits on the critical path of a ground-up build in a way it never does on a small project, so we plan around it from preconstruction rather than absorbing the delay later. Summer heat is a real scheduling factor for concrete and envelope work and is planned for rather than pushed through.

  • Site conditions and access assessed in preconstruction
  • Inspection scheduling planned into the critical path
  • Weather factored into concrete and envelope sequencing
